AAIL: Lifting Solid Geometry Off the Page

In K-12 solid geometry the hard part isn't the arithmetic — it's building the space in your head. AAIL parses a problem into geometric parameters and animation timing so students can rotate and take the solid apart themselves.

RoleProduct design + front-end
AudienceK-12 solid geometry
StackThree.js · LLM parsing
FormInteractive animated learning system

01The block is spatial imagination, not calculation

Watching students work through solid geometry, one thing stood out: their algebra was fine, and almost every stall happened at "which face is this auxiliary line on" or "how exactly do these two planes intersect". A 2D page is inherently lossy for 3D relations, and a textbook can only offer one fixed projection.

So the goal was never to explain the solution — it was to give the lost dimension back to the student.

02Approach: problem → parameters → animation timing

  1. Use an LLM to parse the natural-language problem into structured geometric parameters (points, lines, planes, constraints)
  2. Turn the solution steps into animation timing, one figure change per step
  3. Render a rotatable, explodable model in Three.js, generating auxiliary lines step by step rather than all at once

"Step by step" is the critical design here. Draw every auxiliary line at once and the student still faces one complex finished diagram; revealed in sequence, they can follow why each line exists.

03My part

I owned product design and front-end implementation: defining the output schema of problem parsing, designing the granularity rules for animation steps, and building the Three.js scene and interactions (rotate, explode, step forward/back).

What I tuned repeatedly was granularity — too coarse and students can't follow; too fine and it degrades into mechanically clicking next. There's no standard answer for that balance; you find it by testing real problems one at a time.

Below is the running product: a solid-geometry problem parsed into parameters, then broken into steps a student can turn and replay themselves.

05Retrospective

The real difficulty in education products is step granularity, not rendering quality.

I expected 3D rendering to be the hard part; it turned out to be table stakes, and what actually determined learning outcomes was "how much belongs in one step". That is the same instinct I later used in strategy work: cutting a big problem into right-sized pieces matters more than polishing each piece.

This project hasn't been validated at teaching scale yet. Happy to walk through the parsing schema or the animation breakdown of a specific problem.
