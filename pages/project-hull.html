Hull Tactical: Stability First, Score Second

A single model scored well, then collapsed on another window — textbook overfitting. The real work here wasn't tuning; it was building a validation method that survives a change of period.

01A pretty score is not a working signal

Financial time series carry a very low signal-to-noise ratio, so any model can land a beautiful score on a historical window. Our first single model did exactly that: excellent on one period, collapsing on the next.

Once that was clear, the question changed from "how do we raise the score" to "how do we tell whether this score is fake".

02Three-dimension feature design

I built features from three mutually independent angles so they wouldn't all describe the same thing:

  1. Price momentum: trend strength and reversal signals across windows
  2. Volatility structure: volatility level and shifts in its term structure
  3. Macro sentiment: market sentiment indicators

The lower the correlation between the three, the more blending gains later — something to settle during feature design, not to patch up at the stacking stage.

03Time-series cross-validation and stacking

The pivotal move was replacing random splits with time-series cross-validation, strictly keeping training data earlier than validation data to rule out look-ahead leakage. This makes the score visibly uglier — and the uglier score is the credible one.

On that base we stacked: several base models each modelling a different feature dimension, with a meta-model learning how to weight them. Blending gains come from base-model diversity, which makes the three-dimension feature design a prerequisite.

04Retrospective

Control stability first, then chase the score — that order can't be reversed.

This project changed how I read metrics: whether a number is good depends entirely on the validation it came from. Later, insisting on ground-truth sampling before opening a strategy to full volume came straight from here.
